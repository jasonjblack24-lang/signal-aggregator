# Stale Relationship Review — Playbook-Only Control Test
**Source:** expansion-ready.md playbook + general CSM knowledge  
**Signal data consulted:** None  
**Constraint:** No account-specific data. Reasoning from framework and CSM experience only.

---

## The Starting Problem

Fourteen accounts, each with a 45-plus-day CSM gap. The gap alone tells you almost nothing useful. What it tells you is that you have a prioritization problem, not fourteen identical problems.

The first instinct is to treat the gap as the risk signal and start working through the list. That instinct is wrong. A 70-day gap on an account where every product is growing, the champion initiates contact, and the renewal is twelve months out is not the same problem as a 70-day gap on an account where sentiment has been trending negative for two quarters, support tickets are escalating, and the renewal lands in six weeks. The gap is the reason to look; the underlying signals determine what you're looking at.

The work, before you pick up the phone, is to sort.

---

## How to Sort

Before any outreach, you want to answer three questions for each account:

**1. What direction is the product usage moving?**  
Usage trend is the most reliable leading indicator of where the relationship is heading. Accounts where utilization is increasing — especially across multiple contracted products, especially above baseline — are signaling business health regardless of how long you've been absent. Accounts where utilization is declining, particularly where the decline is broad (not one product, all products) and sustained (not a one-week dip, a consistent 90-day trend), are signaling something has changed in the customer's world that hasn't surfaced in your relationship yet.

**2. What is the relationship infrastructure?**  
Is the champion still active? Is the executive sponsor reachable? Have there been any customer-initiated contacts during the gap, even if just end-user support touches? A gap with active relationship contacts is categorically different from a gap with dormant contacts at every level. The former means you drifted; the latter means the relationship has been cooling for longer than the gap metric captures.

**3. What is the external context?**  
Has anything significant happened in the customer's business during the gap? Funding announcements, leadership changes, competitive threats, product launches, public outages — any of these shifts the nature of the conversation you need to have. Showing up to a funding announcement talking about last quarter's utilization metrics is a missed signal. Showing up after a public outage without acknowledging it is worse.

Those three data points — usage direction, relationship status, external events — will sort fourteen accounts into a much shorter list of things that actually need to happen today.

---

## The Categories You Will Find

In a book of fourteen stale accounts, you will typically find four types.

---

### Type 1: Growing and Neglected

**What the signals look like:** Utilization trending up, at or above contracted baseline, possibly across multiple products. Champion and executive sponsor still active. Low support load. Sentiment green or yellow but not deteriorating. The customer may have initiated contact during the gap — not out of distress, but because they had a question or were doing something new.

**Why the gap happened:** These accounts rarely escalate, rarely complain, and rarely generate urgency. They are easy to deprioritize in favor of accounts that are visibly on fire. The risk is not that they will churn; it is that the CSM relationship becomes transactional or invisible during exactly the period when it has the most value.

**What makes this category interesting from an expansion standpoint:** The expansion-ready playbook is explicit about this: the timing, size, and shape of expansion is heavily influenced by whether the CSM shows up at the right moment with the right framing. An account that is growing above contracted baseline is signaling capacity for more — but a reactive expansion triggered by procurement is worth less than a proactive one shaped by a CSM who understood the growth trajectory before the customer ran out of runway.

**The entry point conversation:** Not a QBR. Not a renewal preview. A genuine, curiosity-led conversation about what's driving the growth. What changed in their product? What's the team building? What are they seeing in their data? The conversation confirms the CSM's hypothesis about the driver, surfaces new contacts who have been doing the work, and creates the context from which a natural expansion conversation can emerge — without the CSM having to manufacture urgency that doesn't exist.

**One caution the playbook names explicitly:** Don't fire an expansion conversation on a growth signal unless you have a correlating signal that confirms the growth is business-driven. A usage spike with no human signal is ambiguous — it might be a misconfiguration or a test environment, not a production scale-up. Showing up with an expansion hypothesis that turns out to be instrumentation noise erodes exactly the trust you're trying to leverage.

---

### Type 2: Declining and Disengaged

**What the signals look like:** Utilization trending down across most or all contracted products. Champion inactive or unresponsive. Executive sponsor may be the only remaining active contact, or may also be silent. Support tickets elevated, possibly escalating in severity. Last CSM touch was customer-initiated, not CSM-initiated — and often it was an end-user reaching out about a problem, not a strategic conversation.

**Why the gap happened:** Sometimes the CSM fell off the account; sometimes the account pulled back. It doesn't matter which came first. What matters is that the account is now in a state where the product is shrinking in their business and there is no relationship presence to understand why.

**What is actually at risk:** Not just renewal. The risk is that by the time the renewal conversation happens, the customer has already decided and is just going through the motions. Declining utilization that has been unaddressed for 60 to 90 days becomes a narrative in the customer's mind — the product isn't working for us, or we scaled back, or we found another way — and that narrative calcifies without anyone from the vendor's side to offer an alternative interpretation or identify what actually changed.

**The entry point conversation:** This one requires care. Do not lead with the utilization data. Do not open with "we noticed your usage has declined." That conversation signals that the CSM is monitoring dashboards rather than paying attention to the customer as a business. The right opener is curious and open: check in on the team, ask what their current priorities look like, find out if anything has changed in the last few months. The goal of the first call is to understand the situation, not to propose a solution. You are probably walking into something that has a context — a reorg, a project pivot, a budget constraint, a performance issue the team has been dealing with in support tickets — and you need that context before you can offer anything useful.

**The harder question:** Is there a champion to call? Accounts in this category often have inactive champions, which means the CSM doesn't have a natural reentry point. If the executive sponsor is still reachable, start there — but frame it as a relationship check-in, not a problem-solving session. If both contacts have gone dark, the gap has become a structural problem, not just a scheduling one.

---

### Type 3: Sentiment Deteriorating but Product Still Present

**What the signals look like:** Utilization declining or volatile, but products are still contracted and in use. The tell is in the support queue: tickets have been elevated, maybe escalating, and the category suggests accumulated frustration (performance issues, configuration problems that haven't resolved, feature requests that indicate the product isn't doing what the customer expected). Sentiment trajectory is drifting toward red. The champion may still be active — they haven't given up, but they're frustrated.

**Why this is distinct from Type 2:** In Type 2, the account has quietly retreated. In Type 3, the account is still engaged but something is wrong and it hasn't been addressed. The champion is still present, which is both an asset and a vulnerability — an active champion who is frustrated and feels unheard will become the customer's strongest internal voice for switching if the situation doesn't improve.

**The entry point conversation:** Acknowledge before you ask for anything. The customer's recent interactions with you have been support tickets and unresolved frustration. Showing up with a product conversation or a renewal preview before you've addressed that backlog signals that the CSM hasn't been paying attention. Start by understanding the current state — specifically the open support issues and what the team has been experiencing — and demonstrate that you've reviewed the history before calling. The goal of the first conversation in this category is to rebuild credibility, not to advance a commercial motion.

---

### Type 4: Gap but No Signal Either Way

**What the signals look like:** Utilization stable, not growing and not declining. Sentiment flat — not positive, not negative, just absent. Champion still nominally active. Low support volume. No external events. The account exists but hasn't generated a signal in either direction during the gap.

**What this category actually is:** These are accounts where the gap is the primary flag and everything else is benign. The relationship has drifted, but nothing has broken, and nothing particularly interesting has happened.

**Why you cannot ignore it:** The absence of a negative signal is not health. It may reflect genuine stability — a team that found their usage pattern and is satisfied with it — or it may reflect disengagement that hasn't manifested in product behavior yet. The difference matters and you cannot tell from the data alone.

**The entry point conversation:** Low intensity. Close the gap with a genuine check-in that doesn't manufacture urgency: how is the team, what's coming up in the next quarter, anything we should know about. The goal is to re-establish that the relationship exists, update your understanding of where the account is heading, and confirm whether there is anything latent — frustration, questions, emerging use cases — that hasn't surfaced in the data.

---

## How to Prioritize Across the Four Types

The rough prioritization order:

**First:** Any Type 2 or Type 3 account with a renewal inside 90 days. The combination of deteriorating relationship health and near-term renewal is the only genuinely time-sensitive configuration in a stale-accounts review. Everything else has runway.

**Second:** Type 2 and Type 3 accounts more broadly, starting with those where the decline is most sustained and the relationship contacts are most inactive. The earlier you engage, the more runway you have to actually change the trajectory. Calling a deteriorating account at 30 days before renewal is not a save; it is a negotiation.

**Third:** Type 1 accounts approaching renewal within 90 days. The gap is not a risk in the same sense, but the renewal window is when expansion conversations need to happen and a CSM who shows up for the first time in 70 days to talk about contract terms is not going to have the relationship context to make that conversation go well.

**Fourth:** Type 1 accounts with active external signals — funding rounds, product launches, leadership changes during the gap. These create natural, non-awkward reentry points that don't require manufacturing a reason to call.

**Fifth:** Type 4 accounts, ordered by ARR and renewal proximity. Low urgency, but the gap still needs to close.

**Lowest priority:** Type 1 accounts that are growing, have stable relationships, renewal is distant, and no external events. These are your healthiest accounts. They deserve attention — eventually — but they are not the reason to come into work early this week.

## What the Expansion Playbook Contributes Here — and What It Doesn't

The expansion-ready playbook is built for Type 1 accounts. It is specific and useful: it tells you which signals constitute a genuine expansion hypothesis (sustained utilization above baseline, corroborated by a human signal or external event), how to route ambiguous signals (recheck before acting), and why firing an expansion conversation prematurely is worse than waiting (the "friend who only calls when they want something" failure mode).

What the playbook does not address: the risk side of a stale account review. Types 2, 3, and 4 are outside its scope. That is not a gap in the playbook — it is a scope decision — but it means a CSM who relies only on this playbook to work a list of stale accounts will be well-equipped to handle the accounts that need expansion conversations and under-equipped to handle the accounts that are actually in trouble.

The honest framing of what the playbook gives you: a rigorous method for not mis-firing on expansion outreach, and a principled reason to show up to a growing account with something other than a renewal agenda. For a stale account review, that covers approximately three of the fourteen accounts. The other eleven require a different set of instincts.

## Portfolio Summary

If I inherited fourteen stale accounts tomorrow, I would expect to find roughly:

- **Two to three** that are genuinely growing and represent real expansion opportunities — the playbook applies directly here, and the work is to confirm the hypothesis before calling.
- **Three to four** that are declining in meaningful ways, with some combination of inactive contacts, escalating support, and negative sentiment — these are the priority calls, not the expansion candidates.
- **Two to three** that have accumulated frustration that hasn't broken the relationship yet but will if it doesn't get addressed — these need acknowledgment before anything else.
- **Five to six** that are flat and benign — the gap exists but nothing is broken; these close the list.

The single most useful thing a CSM can do with a list like this before making any calls is to resist treating it as a queue. It is not a queue. It is a sorting problem, and the sorting — which accounts are actually in distress, which are opportunities, which are fine — is where most of the value lives. An hour of honest signal review before any outreach will produce better outcomes across all fourteen accounts than working the list chronologically and hoping context emerges during the calls.

The second most useful thing: be honest about which category each account is in before you decide what to say. An account in Type 3 that you approach with a Type 1 conversation will notice. An account in Type 2 that gets a growth-focused check-in will feel misread. The framework is only as good as the accuracy with which you apply it.
